🧠 How to learn the grid

A square is a letter a–h and a number 1–8. Think of the letter as a street and the number as a floor. White's side is always at the bottom, with a1 at bottom left.

12-week coaching plan

Weeks 1–2: square coordinates, legal moves and fun miniature games. Weeks 3–4: checks, captures and finding free pieces. Weeks 5–6: forks, pins and one-move checkmates. Weeks 7–8: king safety, castling, promotion and simple king-and-pawn games on a physical board. Weeks 9–10: two-move prediction, openings for principles rather than memorized lines. Weeks 11–12: full games and reviewing one mistake and one good move each time.

Every week: one easy game with Dad and one untimed review. When accuracy drops, revisit the weak technique instead of rushing to unlock levels.

How to spot real improvement

Look for fewer hanging pieces, better threat prediction, safe castling, fewer repeated mistakes and success on previously unseen boards. Add occasional real-board games and child-friendly tournament experience.

👪 Parent guide: how to build a real chess thinker

Before most moves, gently ask: “What is my opponent threatening? What are my checks, captures and threats? If I move here, what could happen next?” Praise clear thinking, curiosity and recovery from mistakes rather than just winning.

What the five worlds teach

Days 1–7: piece superpowers and board setup.
Days 8–15: captures, check, checkmate and special rules.
Days 16–20: tactical vision — forks, pins, skewers and mating patterns.
Days 21–25: good opening habits — center, development and king safety.
Days 26–30: threat detection, endgames and a final coached opening challenge.

What to do after day 30

Play regular untimed games, repeat missed puzzles, practise simple mates, and review one good move and one possible improvement after each game. A formal FIDE master title requires qualifying tournament results or ratings, not completion of this game.
